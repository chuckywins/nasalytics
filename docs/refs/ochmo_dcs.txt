Decompression Sickness Mechanism

Decompression sickness can be understood by the basic gas law principle described by Henry's
Law, which states that the amount of gas dissolved in a liquid is directional proportional to the
partial pressure of that gas above the liquid.

                = 

C= concentration of dissolved gas

P= partial pressure of gas

At cabin pressure, historically 14.7 psia, nitrogen equilibrates between the lungs and blood,
dissolving in plasma and diffusing into the tissues. At the higher pressure, each breath contains
more compressed air molecules resulting in a buildup of nitrogen, a physiologically inert gas.
When ambient pressure is rapidly reduced, (e.g., transitioning from cabin pressure to an EVA
suit, historically 4.3 psia) the partial pressure of nitrogen falls rapidly, and this sudden
equilibrium disruption forces an increase rate of nitrogen elimination. If the off-gassing exceeds
the rate of elimination via exhalation, nitrogen starts to form bubbles in blood and tissues
which is where the manifestation of DCS symptoms occur. Bubbles may appear in extravascular
(tissue or interstitial) and intravascular compartments. There are two main types of
intravascular bubbles:

Pathophysiology

                                 Although the pathophysiology of DCS is not entirely understood, it includes

                                 right to left shunting of VGE via several mechanisms, one of which is a patent

                                 foramen ovale (PFO). A PFO is a shunt between the right and left atrium of the

                                 heart and is a remnant feature of the natal heart. All humans are born with a

                                 PFO, but approximately 75% of the PFOs fuse following childbirth (Steiner,

                                 1998). With the remaining 25% of the population whose PFO does not fuse,

                                 approximately 6% are considered to be large (>2 mm) and the diameter can

                                 also increase in age (Hagen, 1984). A PFO raises concern because theoretically

                                 a VGE may pass from the right to left atrium, bypassing normal lung filtration,

                                 which can allow bubbles in the arterial system leading to a neurological event.

                                 Currently, NASA does not screen out individuals with PFOs. For more

Predisposing Risk

DCS has many predisposing factors such as hydration, body fat %, and exercise type.

Hydration
Hydration plays a significant role in DCS
risk. Dehydration reduces plasma volume
and can increase blood viscosity, leading to
impaired inert gas transport and decreasing
bubble removal from the body. Conversely,
proper hydration promotes effective gas
washout and reduces DCS.

                                                                                            Wang et al., 2020

                                               Exercise
                                               Pre-exposure exercise promotes removal of
                                               inert gasses by increasing circulation and
                                               tissue perfusion. Contrarily, vigorous or
                                               resistive exercises after decompression can
                                               exacerbate bubble growth by promoting
                                               tissue microtrauma and increasing
                                               perfusion gradients.

Conkin et al., 2013

Body Fat Percentage
Higher body fat contents can increase DCS
risk because nitrogen is significantly more
soluble in fat than in water. Higher nitrogen
contents stored in fat leads to excessive
nitrogen bubble formation thus increasing
DCS risk.

DCS Detection

DCS is typically diagnosed based on symptom presentation because the presence of VGE or AGE
does not necessarily indicate DCS. Many individuals have detectable VGE's without symptoms,
while others may have symptoms without detectable bubbles. Generally, low bubble loads are
associated with lower DCS risk and high bubbles are associated with higher risk, but DCS risk
associated with bubble count accuracy is location dependent where the subclavian sites are more
accurate compared to precordial sites. The bubble load (quantity and persistence of bubbles) may
be used to indicate decompression stress, therefore potential DCS risk can be identified using the
Eftedal-Brubakk (EB) scale.

Treatment

Recompression therapy by hyperbaric therapy (HBOT) is the terrestrial treatment for DCS and the
U.S Navy Protocols are widely adopted treatment methods. The U.S. Navy Treatment Tables (TT)
involve administering 100% oxygen at increasing pressures within a hyperbaric chamber to:
· Reduce bubble volume in the body
· Increase absorption of bubbles into the body fluid and exhalation of inert gas from the lungs
· Increase oxygen supply to cells in peripheral tissue
In addition to this treatment, oral or intravenous administration of electrolytes are recommended
for DCS, except for pulmonary DCS cases. Also, for AGE treatment, intravenous or intramuscular
administration of lidocaine is shown to be useful for brain function recovery.

While a hyperbaric chamber is not available during spaceflight, crewmembers exhibiting DCS
symptoms may undergo a treatment similar in principle to the Navy table treatments. Increasing
the pressure in the suit and cabin pressure may be combined to resolve symptoms as a minimum
of 16.4 psia may alleviate symptoms but the protocol is scenario dependent. Below is a terrestrial
decision tree for DCS.

Prevention (continued)

 2. EVA Workload
        High intensity workload especially involving the lower body during an EVA due to increased
        perfusion in large muscle groups, such as ambulation, can promote bubble formation in tissues
        due to increased circulation. Proper workload management is critical in DCS risk management.

 3. Suit Pressure
        In-suit pressure determines the differential pressure between the habitat which directly impacts
        DCS risk and prebreathe duration. Higher suit pressures reduce DCS risk, but limits dexterity and
        endurance while lower suit pressures significantly improve mobility but has an increased risk of
        nitrogen bubble formation.

                     Garbino et al., 2023

4. Preabreathe Duration
      To mitigate DCS risk in spaceflight, a prebreathe (PB) protocol of variable amounts of time is used
      prior to a decompression event. PB works by eliminating nitrogen inhalation in ambient air by
      replacing it with 100% oxygen, therefore reducing alveolar nitrogen partial pressure. With alveolar
      partial pressure nearing zero, nitrogen from tissues diffuse across the gradient between alveoli and
      tissue and is then mobilized into the circulation and subsequently expired, progressively decreasing
      total body nitrogen. Over time, this reduces supersaturation at the time of decompression by
      limiting bubble production, therefore reducing the overall DCS risk. The effectiveness of PB is
      dependent on maintaining a zero-nitrogen alveolar gradient which means any mask leak, seal
      breach, or glove/suit configuration issue that allows the crewmember to inhale ambient cabin air is
      classified as a break in prebreathe (BiPB). Even brief BiPB rapidly reintroduces nitrogen into arterial
      blood, disrupting washout and elevating bubble growth risk. Operationally, a BiPB imposes steep
      compensatory PB time or can force a complete protocol restart, putting the entire EVA timeline at
      risk.

Quantifying DCS Stress

 NASA utilizes both empirical tissue tension models and biophysical bubble growth models to quantify
 decompression stress, design prebreathe protocols, and predict true physiological nitrogen washout.
 The majority of cases present themselves between 60-240 minute and inflammatory responses can last
 greater than 24 hours.

       Garbino et al., 2026

1. The Tissue Ratio (R-value), first used by Haldane at the end of the 19th century to define diver ascent
limits, is used to quantify decompression stress by evaluating nitrogen tension relative to suit ambient
pressure across tissue compartments with varying half-lives (1/2) (Conkin 1994, Conkin et al. 1998).
This method estimates how much nitrogen gas could potentially come out multiple tissue
compartments with different half-lives when pressure changes. Acceptable R-values vary by suit
pressure, historically an EMU suit at 4.3 psi has an R  1.65.

Denitrogenation Prebreathe

To define the true dose through diffusion-based physics and mass-balance, NASA uses the

Tissue Bubble Dynamics Probability (TBDM) model, a biophysical model of bubble growth. This

model applies mathematical and statistical analyses to estimate the likelihood and severity of

bubble formation in tissues. When inputting relevant durations, rates, pressures and gas

compositions, the model outputs a bubble growth index (BGI). The BGI is the time varying ratio

of bubble radius to an initial 3-µm radius of the bubble. This is calculated throughout the

exposure, and the peak value is typically used as the primary measure of decompression stress.

BGI provides significant prediction of DCS and VGE data (p < 0.1). While TBDM models offer

valuable predictive insights, they exhibit substantial variability and are validated through human

testing.

Over the years, NASA has tested and validated various protocols:

                              Past NASA Decompression Protocols for Prevention of DCS
                                    NASA has never experienced a Type II event in spaceflight

           In-Suit 4-Hour Prebreathe
                Astronaut breathes 100% O2 in the spacesuit at 14.7 psia for 4 hours.

           Campout Protocol
                Significantly reduces the required in-suit prebreathe duration by having EVA crewmembers
                "camp out" in the ISS airlock at 10.2 psia, 26.5% O2 during the night prior to their EVA. For
                various operational reasons, the time at 10.2 psia is limited to 8 hours and 40 minutes.

           Exercise Protocol
                Intense, short exercise regimen at 14.7 psia while breathing 100% O2 combined with in-suit
                prebreathe at 10.2 psia.

           In-Suit Light Exercise (ISLE) Protocol
                A longer period of mild exercise in the EMU. The ISLE protocol shares many steps with the
                exercise prebreathe protocol but differs in that 40 minutes are spent breathing 100% O2 by
                mask followed by a 20-minute depressurization to 10.2 psia.

Statistical Validation Framework (Bayesian vs Frequentist) of Validating PB Protocols

 Ground chamber validation testing of prebreathe protocols must satisfy the statistical
 confidence criteria outlined in NASA-STD-3001 [V2 6008].

      [V2 6008] Each program shall define mission unique DCS mitigation strategies to achieve less
      than 4% probability of DCS occurrence with 95% confidence by using the following ground
      chamber test criteria:
      a. No serious DCS in ground exposures

      b. VGE shall be assessed to determine decompression stress.

  To validate that a prebreathe protocol limits DCS risk to < 4% probability with 95%
  statistical confidence (assuming 0 DCS events occur during ground chamber testing):

  Frequentist Calculation ( = 74)
  · Assumption: Zero prior knowledge. The protocol is treated as a complete unknown,

        evaluating only the new trial data.
  · Drawback: Requires 74 human exposures in a hypobaric chamber per protocol

        which is resource-intensive and operationally slow.
  Bayesian Calculation ( = 47)
  · Assumption: Incorporates prior evidence from decades of historical NASA/U.S. Air

        Force hypobaric chamber trials, physiological models, and ISS flight data (e.g., ISLE
        and Campout protocols).
  · Benefit: Reduces human subject exposure requirements by 27 fewer chamber tests
        while meeting the exact same 95% statistical confidence threshold.

Future Options

 NASA is currently exploring alternative parameters and conditions through the Exploration
 Atmosphere (EA) study to shorten the amount of time spent on PB. These test simulate the
 pressure transition during cabin to suited pressure changes, and the results focus on nitrogen
 washout efficiency, DCS risk mitigation, and operational feasibility. The results from these test will
 inform the Artemis mission, suit design constraints, and risk posture to further ensure prebreathe
 protocols are both effective and efficient.

Future Option: Working PB

The Working (or "Step-Down") Prebreathe Protocol is a staged decompression strategy
designed to mitigate the risk of DCS during EVAs. Unlike resting PB, the step-down approach
pairs an elevated initial spacesuit pressure with active surface task execution, incrementally
stepping down suit pressure throughout the duration of the EVA. By beginning the EVA at a
higher pressure, initial decompression stress and bubble growth risk are kept low when tissue
nitrogen is at its peak. As the crew member actively works on 100% O2, metabolic gas exchange
accelerates denitrogenation, allowing suit pressure to be stepped down in stages.

    pressure, humidity, temperature, ppO2, and ppCO2 data to the crew locally and remotely.

[V2 7043] Medical Capability A medical system shall be provided to the crew to meet the medical

requirements of NASA-STD-3001, Volume 1.

[V2 9053] Protective Equipment Protective equipment shall be provided to protect the crew from
    expected hazards.

[V2 11100] Pressure Suits for Protection from Cabin Depressurization The system shall provide the
    capability for crewmembers to wear pressure suits for sufficient duration during launch, entry, descent
    (to/from Earth, or other celestial body) and any operation deemed high risk for loss of crew life due to
    loss of cabin pressurization (such as in mission dockings, operations during periods of high incidence
    of Micrometeoroids and Orbital Debris (MMOD) or complex vehicle maneuvers).

[V2 11032] LEA Suited Decompression Sickness Prevention Capability LEA spacesuits shall be capable of
    operating at sufficient pressure to protect against Type II decompression sickness in the event of a
    cabin depressurization.

[V2 11036] Suited Atmospheric Monitoring and Alerting Suits shall monitor suit pressure, ppO2, and
    ppCO2 and alert the crewmember when they are outside safe limits.
